Dates are one of those topics that look trivial until the first bug report from a user in a different
country. The good news is that a few consistent rules prevent almost all of the pain.

## Two different things called "a date"

The root of most confusion is that there are two separate concepts:

- **An instant**: a single moment in time, the same for everyone on Earth. "The order was placed at
  12:00 UTC on 3 March."
- **A calendar date or wall-clock time**: something people read off a calendar or clock, meaningful
  only in a place. "My birthday is 3 March." "The shop opens at 09:00."

Instants should be stored as instants. A birthday is not an instant at all: it has no time zone, and
storing it as midnight in some zone is how people end up with birthdays one day off.

## Rule 1: store and transmit in UTC

Keep instants in UTC in your database and APIs, and use the unambiguous ISO 8601 format with an explicit
offset:

```text
2026-03-03T12:00:00Z
```

The trailing `Z` means UTC. Strings like `03/04/2026` are ambiguous (is that March or April?) and
strings without an offset leave the reader guessing which zone you meant. A Unix timestamp is fine too,
as long as everyone agrees whether it is in seconds or milliseconds.

## Rule 2: convert only at the edges

Do arithmetic and comparisons on UTC values, and convert to a person's local zone as the very last step
before showing it. In JavaScript, `Intl.DateTimeFormat` does this properly:

```ts
const instant = new Date("2026-03-03T12:00:00Z");

new Intl.DateTimeFormat("en-GB", {
  dateStyle: "medium",
  timeStyle: "short",
  timeZone: "Asia/Kolkata",
}).format(instant);
// "3 Mar 2026, 17:30"
```

Always use named zones from the IANA database (`Europe/Dublin`, `America/New_York`), never a fixed
offset such as `+01:00`. A named zone knows when daylight saving starts and ends; a fixed offset does
not, and will be an hour wrong for half the year.

## Rule 3: be careful with "days"

Not every day has 24 hours. On the days clocks change, a local day is 23 or 25 hours long, so "add 24
hours" and "add one day" are different operations. Likewise, "start of today" depends entirely on whose
today you mean.

When you need "all events on 3 March", decide whose 3 March it is, compute the start and end of that day
in that zone, convert both to UTC, and query the range between them.

## JavaScript's own traps

- `new Date("2026-03-03")` is parsed as UTC midnight, while `new Date("2026-03-03T00:00:00")` is parsed
  as *local* midnight. The same-looking inputs give different instants.
- `getMonth()` is zero-based (January is `0`) and `getDate()` is one-based, which catches everyone at
  least once.
- A `Date` object is always an instant; it does not carry a time zone. The zone only appears when you
  format it.
- For anything beyond simple formatting, consider a dedicated library or the newer `Temporal` API, which
  separates instants, calendar dates and zoned times into distinct types, exactly the distinction above.

## Testing the awkward cases

If your software deals with time in a way that matters, test it against the situations that break naive
code: a user in a zone far from yours, the day clocks go forward and the day they go back, the end of a
month, a leap year, and midnight. Most date bugs live in those corners, and a handful of fixed-date unit
tests will find them cheaply.

## The short version

Store instants in UTC, label them with an explicit offset, use named IANA zones, convert at the edges,
and treat calendar dates as a separate concept from moments in time. Follow those and your data stays
correct no matter where your users are.
